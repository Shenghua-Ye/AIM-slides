Hi everyone, I am Shenghua. Today’s paper is AIM, Scalable Pre-training of Large Autoregressive Image Models, from Apple, at ICML 2024. The question: can a vision model learn the way GPT does — just by predicting what comes next — and keep getting better as it grows? AIM learns visual features this way, and the features are then tested on image classification. Here are three questions to keep in mind; the answers come during the talk.

Motivation
Motivation: can vision be trained the way GPT is?
next-token prediction scales   ·   DINOv2 and MAE scale less cleanly (§2)
AIM borrows GPT’s recipe for vision — does it scale the same way?
3 / 9
First, the motivation. GPT learns from one simple task: read the text so far, and predict the next word. The cat sat on the… mat. And this simple task scales. Make the model bigger and give it more data, and the loss goes down and the model gets better, in a predictable way. According to the authors, vision does not have this yet. DINOv2, the strongest self-supervised model, relies on many training tricks and does not show the scaling law we see in language. And in one large study, masked models like MAE gained little from billions of images. The idea itself is not new: iGPT tried it in 2020. AIM revisits it with modern tools and asks: if we train a vision model the GPT way — predict the next patch, with a plain recipe, on web-scale data — does it scale?
Method · 1 of 4 · next-patch prediction
Cut the image into patches, then predict them in order
224×224 image → 256 patches   ·   Table 3b
Read the patches row by row; at each step, predict the next patch’s pixels.
4 / 9
How do you turn an image into something like a sentence? Cut it into small square patches. In AIM, a 224 by 224 image becomes 256 patches, each 14 by 14 pixels. Then read them in a fixed order, row by row, like reading a page. [Click: Step a few times] At every position, the model sees all the patches before it, and predicts the pixels of the next one. The coloured part is what the model has seen; the orange square is what it must predict. [Click: Random] They also tried other orders, such as spiral and random. Row by row was the best: 77.4 on ImageNet, against 75.7 for random. Their explanation: in raster order the task stays hard all the way through, so the model has to keep more information.
Method · 2 of 4 · the model
A Transformer trunk, plus a heavy head that is thrown away
12-block MLP head   ·   normalised-pixel loss
The head does the pixel work, then is thrown away — only the trunk is kept.
5 / 9
Here is the model. The patches go into a standard Vision Transformer, called the trunk. On top sits a head: a stack of twelve MLP blocks that turns each patch feature into predicted pixels. The loss is the squared error between the predicted and the true pixels of the next patch, normalised per patch. Two details matter. First, the head is thrown away after pre-training; only the trunk is used later. The authors’ hypothesis: the head absorbs the low-level pixel work, so the trunk can learn more general features. Without the head, the ImageNet score drops from 78.2 to 75.4. Second, the training is plain: no special tricks to keep it stable, and the same settings for every model size — just like training an LLM.
Method · 3 of 4 · prefix attention
Prefix attention: train left-to-right, then look both ways
Figure 3   ·   Table 3d
A random prefix looks both ways and is not predicted; the rest is causal.
6 / 9
Now a problem. To predict the next patch, each patch may only look at the patches before it. That is a causal mask, the same as in GPT. [Click: Causal] This grid shows who may look at whom. Each row is a patch, and orange marks the patches it is allowed to attend to. On the right is what that row’s output must predict: always the next patch. But for classification we want every patch to see the whole image: bidirectional attention. [Click: Downstream] If you pre-train with a causal mask and then remove it, the model is confused: its ImageNet score falls from 77.4 to 52.3. AIM’s fix is prefix attention. [Click: Prefix] During pre-training, the first few patches — a random number of them — can see each other freely, and they are not predicted. Only the rest is predicted. [Move the slider] So the model learns to work with both kinds of attention. With prefix pre-training and full attention at test time, the score is 78.2, the best of the four.
Method · 4 of 4 · the data
Two billion web images beat one million clean ones
Table 2   ·   captions used only for filtering
Lots of web images beat a small clean set — ImageNet alone overfits.
7 / 9
Next, the data. LLMs are trained on huge amounts of web text, and AIM does the same with images. The authors start from 12.8 billion image–text pairs from the web and keep the 15 percent whose captions match their images best. That gives 2 billion images, called DFN-2B. The captions are only used for this filter: AIM itself never reads text. When AIM trains only on ImageNet, about one million images, the validation loss goes down quickly and then goes up again: it overfits. With the web images it keeps improving. Mixing in a little ImageNet, one sample in five, gives the best result: the average over 15 benchmarks rises from 73.5 to 74.5 to 75.6.
Results · 1 of 2 · scaling
Lower loss, better features — and no plateau yet
Figures 1 and 6   ·   0.6B → 7B parameters
Lower loss goes with better features — and neither curve has flattened.
8 / 9
This is the main result, the paper’s Figure 1. On the left are four model sizes, from 0.6 to 7 billion parameters. The x-axis is the pre-training loss on a validation set; the y-axis is the average accuracy on 15 downstream benchmarks. As the model grows, the loss goes down, and the accuracy goes up with it. So, in these experiments, the loss is a useful signal: watching it tells you whether the features are getting better, as with LLMs. On the right, the same small model is trained on more unique images: 1 million, 100 million, 2 billion. The accuracy keeps rising. And neither curve has flattened. The authors see no sign of saturation, even at 7 billion parameters. One more sign, from the paper’s Figure 6: at similar compute, a smaller model trained for longer reaches about the same loss as a bigger model trained for less time. That is the trade-off Chinchilla found for language models, though the authors leave a full scaling law for future work.

How good are the features? Here the trunk is frozen, and only a small attention-pooling classifier is trained on top. These are averages over 15 benchmarks. AIM at 7 billion parameters reaches 79.6 when we read the right layer. It clearly beats the other generative methods, MAE and BEiT. But to be fair, DINOv2, a joint-embedding method, is still ahead at 81.9. It uses higher-resolution images and many training tricks — and a smaller model. The authors admit that, at the same model size, methods like DINOv2 still learn stronger features; AIM’s strength is that it is simple to scale. Two more findings. First, the best features are not in the last layer. The authors think this is because predicting pixels differs from classification, so the most semantic features need not sit at the top. The best layer is about two-thirds of the way up: on ImageNet-1k, the 7B model gets 84.0 instead of 82.4. Second, a light fine-tune with LoRA, training about 0.1 percent of the weights, reaches 86.3 on ImageNet-1k. To sum up. The motivation: can the GPT recipe work for images? The method: predict the next patch, with a throwaway head, prefix attention and two billion web images. The result: the loss tracks the quality of the features, and it keeps improving with bigger models and more data. Thank you.
